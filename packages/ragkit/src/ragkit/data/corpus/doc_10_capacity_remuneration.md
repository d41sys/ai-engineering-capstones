# Capacity Remuneration Mechanisms and Security of Supply

In energy-only electricity markets, generation assets recover capital expenditures and fixed operational costs exclusively through wholesale energy sales and scarcity pricing spikes. However, the 'missing money problem' often disincentivizes investment in peaking plants that operate only tens of hours per year. Capacity Remuneration Mechanisms (CRMs) address this by providing explicit payments for reliable capacity availability.

## Design Archetypes of Capacity Mechanisms

Capacity mechanisms vary widely across jurisdictions:

1. Capacity Markets: Centralized, auction-based systems (e.g., PJM Reliability Pricing Model, Great Britain Capacity Market) where the system operator forecasts peak demand years ahead (T-4 auctions) and procures capacity obligations. Winners receive fixed payments ($/kW-year) in exchange for maintaining availability during declared system stress events.
2. Strategic Reserves: TSOs contract specific generation units outside the wholesale market (e.g., mothballed gas or coal plants). These units are dispatched solely when wholesale markets fail to clear and emergency load shedding is imminent.
3. Reliability Options: Generators sell financial call options with a predetermined strike price. If market prices exceed the strike price during a scarcity event, the generator refunds the difference to the market, eliminating extreme windfall profits while ensuring revenue stability.

## Derating Factors and De-Carbonization Constraints

Not all nameplate megawatts contribute equally to system security of supply. System operators apply technology-specific derating factors based on Equivalent Firm Capacity (EFC) and loss of load expectation (LOLE) contributions. While thermal gas turbines receive derating factors of 85% to 95%, four-hour battery storage may be derated to 40% to 60%, and variable wind to 10% to 20%. Furthermore, modern emissions performance standards (e.g., 550 g CO2/kWh ceilings in the EU Clean Energy Package) disqualify high-emitting peakers, driving demand for clean demand response and long-duration storage.
## Pay-for-Performance and Availability Penalties

Capacity market agreements impose stringent financial penalties for underperformance during declared system stress hours. Under Pay-for-Performance frameworks, capacity providers that fail to deliver their contracted megawatt capacity during acute grid emergency events face clawback charges that can exceed their cumulative annual capacity revenues. Conversely, assets that over-deliver during stress events earn bonus payments funded by penalty collections. These severe financial disincentives compel asset operators to invest in rigorous preventative maintenance, dual-fuel backup firing capabilities, and cold-weather weatherization packages to ensure operational readiness.
